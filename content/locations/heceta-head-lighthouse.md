Dramatically perched 205 feet above the crashing waves of the Pacific Ocean, Heceta Head Lighthouse is one of the most scenic beacons on the Pacific Coast — and one of the most photographed spots in Oregon. First lit in 1894, during the height of America's lighthouse construction era, this elegant structure combines exceptional engineering with breathtaking natural beauty, and its light still sweeps the sea more than 130 years later.

## Strategic Coastal Location

The selection of Heceta Head for lighthouse construction reflected the U.S. Lighthouse Board's recognition that this prominent headland provided an ideal location for a beacon serving the central Oregon coast. Congress appropriated funds in 1889, and the government purchased land on the headland in 1891.

The headland is named for Bruno de Heceta, the Spanish naval officer who surveyed this coast in 1775; the name was formally applied by U.S. Coast Survey scientist George Davidson in 1862. Long before any lighthouse stood here, the prominent point served mariners as a landmark for fixing their position along the often fog-shrouded Oregon coast.

The lighthouse filled a critical gap in coastal navigation aids. According to the Oregon Encyclopedia, roughly ninety miles of dark, dangerous water separated the lighthouses at Yaquina Head to the north and Cape Arago to the south. Heceta Head's new light closed that gap on one of the Pacific Coast's most treacherous stretches of shoreline.

## Engineering Excellence and Construction

Building Heceta Head Lighthouse required overcoming serious logistical challenges. The site was remote — reachable only by rough coastal routes — so materials had to be brought in by sea and over primitive trails, then worked up the steep headland to the construction site.

Work began in 1892, and the 56-foot masonry tower was completed in August 1893. Its white-painted form and red-roofed outbuildings created an ensemble that combined functional excellence with an aesthetic worthy of its spectacular natural setting, visible for miles along the coast.

## First-Order Fresnel Lens Technology

Heceta Head Lighthouse was equipped with a first-order Fresnel lens — the largest class of lighthouse optic — manufactured by Chance Brothers of Birmingham, England. The lens crossed the ocean and arrived in October 1893 aboard the lighthouse tender *Manzanita*; on March 30, 1894, keepers lit the five-wick kerosene lamp for the first time.

The lens assembly's precisely cut glass prisms, arranged in concentric rings, captured light from the central lamp and focused it into a powerful beam that could penetrate the dense fogs that frequently shroud the Oregon coast. The light is visible about twenty-one miles at sea, and Oregon State Parks describes it today as the brightest beacon on the Oregon coast.

Remarkably, that original optic never left the tower. Oregon State Parks notes it is the only active British-made Chance Brothers lens of its kind in the United States — modern electric lighting now shines through the same Victorian glass that first glowed in 1894.

## Lighthouse Keeper Families

Operating the light required resident keepers, who lived with their families in substantial dwellings built near the tower, including a handsome Queen Anne-style keepers' house that still stands. The first head keeper was Andrew Hald; the last, Oswald Allik, tended the light from 1957 until automation — after years of service on the far harsher Tillamook Rock.

Lighthouse keeping at Heceta Head demanded both technical skill and endurance. Keepers climbed the tower's spiral staircase repeatedly to tend the lamp, clean the great lens, wind the clockwork that rotated it, and maintain the machinery. During storms they remained on duty through the night, keeping the beacon burning when mariners most needed it.

The station was isolated for decades — supplies came in by boat and rough wagon road until improved highways reached the coast in the twentieth century — and keeper families became notably self-reliant, raising gardens and livestock and schooling children on the headland.

## Automation and Historic Preservation

The U.S. Coast Guard automated Heceta Head Lighthouse on July 20, 1963, ending the era of resident keepers while keeping the beacon in service. Unlike many automated lights, Heceta Head retained its original first-order lens, which continues to turn today.

Stewardship of the station is now shared. The lighthouse itself was transferred from the Coast Guard to the Oregon Parks and Recreation Department, which operates it as a private aid to navigation within Heceta Head Lighthouse State Scenic Viewpoint. The surviving assistant keepers' dwelling — listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1978 — stands on U.S. Forest Service land and operates as the Heceta Head Lighthouse Bed and Breakfast under a Forest Service concession, letting overnight guests sample lighthouse living while supporting preservation.

A major restoration of the tower between 2011 and 2013 returned the lighthouse to sound condition, renewing its ironwork, masonry, and finishes for another century of service.

## Photography and Tourism Icon

Heceta Head's combination of dramatic setting and elegant architecture has made it a favorite of photographers — it is frequently described as one of the most photographed lighthouses on the Pacific Coast. Its position on a cliff above the surf, framed by forested headlands and wildflower meadows, creates compositions that have inspired countless photographers and artists, and the view of the lighthouse from Highway 101 to the south is among the most reproduced images of the Oregon coast.

## Visitor Experience

Today, Heceta Head Lighthouse serves as both an active navigation aid and an educational site interpreting Oregon's maritime heritage. Guided tours, offered seasonally and by appointment through Oregon State Parks, climb toward the lantern room while explaining the technical and human history of the station.

Interpretive programs emphasize both the engineering achievement of the Chance Brothers lens and the stories of the families who lived and worked on this isolated headland. For many visitors, the highlight is simple: standing beside a Victorian lighthouse whose original lens still flashes its signature across the Pacific, exactly as it has since 1894.
