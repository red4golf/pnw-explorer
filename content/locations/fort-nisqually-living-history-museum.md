Standing as a careful reconstruction of the first permanent European settlement on Puget Sound, Fort Nisqually Living History Museum transports visitors to 1855, when the Hudson's Bay Company operated this bustling trading and farming post near the Nisqually Delta. Established in 1833, the fort was never a military installation — despite the name, it was a commercial enterprise, all account books and wool bales rather than cannons. This living museum preserves the story of cultural exchange, frontier commerce, and the complex relationships that shaped early Washington Territory.

## Hudson's Bay Company Expansion

In 1833, the Hudson's Bay Company, Britain's powerful fur trading enterprise, recognized the strategic importance of Puget Sound's protected waters and abundant resources. The company established Fort Nisqually near Sequalitchew Creek, in what is now DuPont, as a midway station between its Columbia District headquarters at Fort Vancouver and its posts to the north. The fort took its name from the Nisqually people, who had lived in the region for thousands of years.

Chief Trader Archibald McDonald oversaw the fort's founding in 1833, arriving with a party that included Dr. William Fraser Tolmie, a young Scottish physician and naturalist. In 1843 the company relocated the post to a better-watered site on the plain above the original beachside location, and Tolmie returned that year to take charge, remaining until 1859. Under his management, Fort Nisqually became more than a trading post: it served as headquarters of the Puget Sound Agricultural Company, a Hudson's Bay Company subsidiary formed by 1839, whose farms and vast herds would reshape the region's landscape. (HBC operations in the region as a whole were directed from Fort Vancouver, on the Columbia River to the south.)

## Cultural Crossroads

Fort Nisqually served as a remarkable meeting place where British traders, French-Canadian voyageurs, Native American tribes, and later American settlers interacted in complex networks of trade, diplomacy, and cultural exchange. The Nisqually, Puyallup, and other Coast Salish peoples became essential partners, providing furs, salmon, and local knowledge while adapting to the economic opportunities the fort created.

The post drew its workforce from across the Hudson's Bay Company's far-flung world. French-Canadian voyageurs brought their expertise in wilderness travel and fur handling, Hawaiian laborers crossed the Pacific to work the farms and warehouses, and Scottish and English clerks managed operations and correspondence. Native American employees served as guides, interpreters, herders, and skilled workers essential to the fort's success.

## Agricultural Revolution

As beaver populations declined, Fort Nisqually's future turned from furs to farming. The Puget Sound Agricultural Company established extensive farms, sheep ranges, and cattle operations on the prairies around the fort — by 1845, according to HistoryLink, the enterprise ran more than 2,200 cattle and nearly 5,900 sheep. These operations brought European-style ranching and farming to the Puget Sound country on a large scale, changing the landscape and economy.

The fort's workshops and stores supplied much of what frontier life required, from horseshoes and farm tools to trade goods and provisions. Blacksmiths, carpenters, and other tradesmen kept the post and its farms running, and Fort Nisqually became an important supply point for early settlers arriving on Puget Sound.

## Transition to American Control

The Oregon Treaty of 1846 set the international boundary at the 49th parallel, placing Fort Nisqually within American territory, but the British company's operations continued for another two decades. In 1869 the Hudson's Bay Company and Puget Sound Agricultural Company sold their remaining claims in the area to the United States government, ending the British commercial era on Puget Sound.

The U.S. Army established nearby Fort Steilacoom in 1849 in response to rising tensions between settlers and Native peoples, and the two very different "forts" — one military, one commercial — coexisted through the turbulent 1850s. Fort Nisqually's long trading relationships with local tribes gave it a distinctive position during those conflicts.

## Modern Living History

Today's Fort Nisqually Living History Museum stands not on the original DuPont site but in Tacoma's Point Defiance Park, where the fort was reconstructed in the 1930s by the Works Progress Administration. Two buildings from the original fort — the Factor's House and the Granary — were preserved and moved to the new site, anchoring a reconstruction based on historical documentation. Costumed interpreters recreate life at the fort in 1855, demonstrating blacksmithing, candle making, woodworking, and other period crafts while explaining the social dynamics of a multicultural frontier community.

The museum's educational programs emphasize the diverse perspectives and experiences of all who lived and worked at the fort, from company officers and skilled tradesmen to Native American partners and indentured servants. This inclusive approach helps visitors understand how different cultures adapted to and shaped life on the Pacific Northwest frontier.

Fort Nisqually shows the Pacific Northwest's multicultural heritage and the complex process of building new societies in frontier settings. Its story illuminates themes of cultural exchange, economic transformation, and adaptation that continue to shape the region today.
