# PNW Historical Explorer — writing standard
**The voice: "The witness with a notebook."**
Derived 2026-07-26 by reverse-engineering the two entries Charles hand-wrote — `port-madison.md` (Flesch 70.2) and `bowerman-airport-lanas-cafe.md` (51.0) — against the two least readable entries in the corpus. Every rule below is a description of something those two entries actually do.

## Why these two
- Port Madison's first three sentences run 20, 10, and 41 words and work as misdirection: 'It looks like a place where nothing much ever happened. In fact this was once one of the busiest industrial centers on Puget Sound, the first seat of Kitsap County, and the spot where local settlers decided their new county would not be named for a battlefield death.' The short sentence lowers the reader, the long one pays out three specifics at once.
- The load-bearing sentences are the shortest ones. 'Meigs built fast.' (3 words) 'Fortune ran hot and cold.' (5) 'Each time Meigs rebuilt bigger.' (5) 'The point was the flight.' (5) 'That's gone.' (2) These carry the argument; the long sentences carry the evidence.
- Emotion is an inventory of physical nouns, never an adjective: 'the sound of propellers winding down on the ramp, the smell of salt air mixing with avgas, the warmth of the cafe on a foggy morning, and the stories, always the stories.' Nothing in that list is evaluated. The reader does the feeling.
- Numbers arrive welded to dates and consequences: 'On February 18, 1861, a boiler exploded and wrecked the mill, killing five men.' 'By 1858 the mill was cutting 15,000 board feet a day.' Port Madison carries 24 numerals and 4 full Month-Day-Year dates in 691 words. Mukilteo carries 3 numerals in 1,184 words and zero full dates.
- Superlatives are bounded by domain and date, or attributed to a source: 'the first brass and iron foundry in Washington Territory,' 'the first full-rigged sailing ship built on the Pacific Coast,' 'among the leading plants on the Sound,' and — for the contested one — 'in what one account called one of the hottest political fights in county history.' Every claim is checkable as written.
- Uncertainty is stated out loud rather than smoothed over: 'The exact reasons remain unclear, perhaps rising rents, perhaps changing economics, perhaps simply the natural lifecycle of small businesses.' And 'the coroner ruled the death an accident' — attributing the verdict rather than asserting it. This is the single most transferable accuracy move in the corpus.
- Verbs are physical and past tense. Port Madison uses 29 distinct concrete action verbs in 691 words — bought, moved, cut, shipped, exploded, wrecked, burned, rebuilt, limped, emptied, vanished. Mukilteo and Yakama use one each across 2,270 words; their main verbs are 'served as,' 'represented,' 'reflected,' 'provided.'
- Both exemplars end on a fact that circles back to the opening rather than a thesis: 'And the county that got its name here, on a summer day in 1857, still carries it more than a century and a half later.' The phrase doing the emotional work is 'on a summer day' — a detail, not a judgment.

## The opening

Three sentences, roughly 20 / 10 / 40 words, in that order. First: place the reader physically — bay, bluff, bearing, distance, airport identifier, town — in present tense, with no adjective and no participle. Second: a short flat sentence about what it looks like now, ideally one that lowers expectation ('It looks like a place where nothing much ever happened') or sets up a reversal. Third: turn on 'In fact' or 'But', and pay out the one thing this entry exists to tell, carrying two or three specific nouns, dates, or names in a single run. Do not state the theme, do not use the word 'significance', and do not name the category the place belongs to. Port Madison and Bowerman both do exactly this; Bowerman's turn lands one paragraph later ('But for decades, Bowerman's true claim to fame wasn't its runway specs or instrument approaches. It was Lana's Cafe.'), which is the acceptable variant.

## The rules

**1. Open with something you could stand next to: a name, a compass bearing, a distance, a date. Present tense, no adjective, no participle.**

*Why:* Both exemplars locate the reader physically in sentence one before asking for any attention. It also gives the narrator a clean first breath in audio.

*Example:* 'On a narrow arm of Port Madison Bay, at the north end of Bainbridge Island, sits a quiet neighborhood of waterfront homes.' / 'Bowerman Airport (KHQM) sits just two miles west of Hoquiam, Washington.'

**2. Vary sentence length violently. After any sentence over 30 words, put one under 10.**

*Why:* Sentence-length standard deviation is 9.7 (Port Madison) and 10.2 (Bowerman) against 6.7 and 5.9 in the weak pair. About a third of exemplar sentences are 12 words or shorter; the weak entries contain zero sentences under 13 words. That flatness is what makes them unreadable aloud — there is nowhere to breathe.

*Example:* 'Meigs built fast. By 1858 the mill was cutting 15,000 board feet a day and ranked among the leading plants on the Sound.'

**3. Give every action a named human subject and a plain past-tense verb. If you cannot name who did it, you probably cannot source it either.**

*Why:* Named actors make claims falsifiable, which is the accuracy requirement, and they make sentences move, which is the readability requirement. Same move solves both.

*Example:* 'In 1854 he bought J. J. Felt's sawmill, moved it to Port Madison, and never really left.' Not: 'Community volunteers contributed thousands of hours to restoration efforts.'

**4. Carry emotion with an itemized list of physical things. Delete the adjective that was going to tell the reader how to feel.**

*Why:* Across 1,557 words the two exemplars contain exactly one stock evaluative adjective ('iconic', once). The weak pair contain 23. Specific true detail is the entertainment; adjectives are what you write when you don't have the detail.

*Example:* 'the smell of salt air mixing with avgas' does the work that 'a beloved and charming landmark' fails to do.

**5. Put a number in every paragraph where one exists, with its date attached. Never use an intensity word in place of a count.**

*Why:* 'Thousands of hours,' 'over a century,' 'countless generations' are unsourceable by construction — there is no document that confirms them. An exact figure is both more vivid and more defensible.

*Example:* 'In the first six months of 1870 alone, running day and night, the mill cut and shipped nearly 11.9 million board feet.' Note 'nearly' — the hedge is doing honest work.

**6. Bound or attribute every superlative. Use 'one of the', 'among the', or a stated domain and date. If it is contested, name whose claim it is.**

*Why:* Charles's rule bans flat superlatives; 254 of them sit unattributed across 86 entries. Port Madison shows the fix costs nothing stylistically — it reads better hedged.

*Example:* 'one of the busiest industrial centers on Puget Sound' / 'the first brass and iron foundry in Washington Territory' / 'in what one account called one of the hottest political fights in county history'

**7. Say plainly when the record is thin. Uncertainty is content, not weakness.**

*Why:* This is the sentence pattern that lets the voice stay entertaining and survive an academic sourcing audit at the same time. It converts a gap in the evidence into a moment of texture.

*Example:* 'The exact reasons remain unclear, perhaps rising rents, perhaps changing economics, perhaps simply the natural lifecycle of small businesses.'

**8. Ban 'while' as a connective. Two facts get two sentences.**

*Why:* Zero uses across both exemplars; 14 in Mukilteo and 10 in Yakama. The 'X, while also Y-ing' weld is the single clearest machine signature in the corpus, and in narration it produces a clause chain the listener cannot hold.

*Example:* 'These technological improvements enhanced safety while reducing the isolation that had traditionally characterized lighthouse keeping.' becomes two sentences, each with a subject who did something.

**9. Never let an abstract noun be the subject of a sentence. No 'the establishment of', 'the construction of', 'the introduction of', 'the automation of'.**

*Why:* Abstract-noun density runs 0.9 per 100 words in Port Madison and 3.9 in Bowerman against 11.9 in Mukilteo and 10.5 in Yakama. A nominalized subject hides the actor, which is exactly the sentence shape that lets an unsourced claim slip through unnoticed.

*Example:* 'The construction of Mukilteo Lighthouse directly supported the development of regular ferry service' → 'The lighthouse made the Whidbey ferry run possible in fog and darkness.'

**10. Write section headings that could only belong to this entry, and let them carry a fact or a date.**

*Why:* Ten heading strings repeat across unrelated places in the corpus. A heading like 'Cultural Legacy' tells the reader nothing and proves the entry was assembled from a template.

*Example:* 'The Six Months It Was Slaughter County' / 'The End of an Era (January 2015)' / 'The Slow Fade'. Not: 'Educational Programs', 'Historic Preservation and Community Stewardship'.

**11. Close on a fact that returns to the opening image. Do not tell the reader what it meant.**

*Why:* Port Madison ends with the county name it opened with and stops. Bowerman is the weaker of the two exemplars (Flesch 51 vs 70.2) largely because it ends on a stated moral — 'preserving the soul of general aviation'. Where the two exemplars disagree, follow Port Madison.

*Example:* 'And the county that got its name here, on a summer day in 1857, still carries it more than a century and a half later.'

**12. Address the reader only for a physical experience they could actually have today, and only in an entry where you were there.**

*Why:* Bowerman uses 'you' four times and earns it — Charles is a pilot and the hero photo is from his own first solo cross-country. Port Madison uses it zero times. Second person from a narrator with no standing reads as tour-brochure copy.

*Example:* 'You can still fly to KHQM, still make the approach to Runway 24, still taxi to the parking area. But the smell of fresh coffee and bacon? That's gone.'

## Never do these
- Participial scene-setting openers with an evaluative adjective — 'Perched on a bluff overlooking the scenic waters of Possession Sound' / 'In the heart of Washington's Yakima Valley'. Six such openers in Mukilteo, one in the two exemplars combined. It delays the subject and signals brochure before the first fact arrives.
- 'stands as' / 'serves as' / 'represents' / 'reflects' / 'provides' as the main verb. Mukilteo and Yakama use these 16 and 18 times per thousand words. They assert significance instead of showing evidence, and nothing in them can be fact-checked.
- The 'while' weld ('X improved safety while reducing isolation'). 24 uses across the two weak entries, 0 across the exemplars. Unspeakable aloud, and it buries the second claim where no one audits it.
- Abstract nouns as sentence subjects — 'The establishment of the ferry route represented a significant improvement in transportation reliability.' The actor vanishes, so the claim has no owner and no source.
- Stock evaluative adjectives: beloved, charming, remarkable, crucial, iconic, profound, essential, comprehensive, spectacular, extraordinary, enduring, distinctive, sophisticated. Present 23 times in the weak pair, once in the exemplars. Each one is an unsourced editorial verdict wearing a description's clothes.
- Metronome paragraphs. 20 of Mukilteo's 23 paragraphs are exactly two sentences; 15 of Yakama's 19 are. Exemplar paragraphs run 1 to 6 sentences with no repeating pattern. Uniformity is the most audible tell in narration.
- Reusable section headings ('Educational Programs', 'Cultural Legacy', 'Community Integration'). They appear across unrelated entries and prove the shape came before the research.
- Unnamed collective actors doing unquantified good — 'Local residents took pride', 'Community volunteers contributed thousands of hours', 'Community involvement created strong bonds'. Warm, unfalsifiable, unsourceable, and factually empty.
- A closing thesis that explains the significance — 'Mukilteo Lighthouse shows the importance of community-scale maritime infrastructure and the enduring value of preserving local landmarks.' It is advocacy, it is unverifiable, and it lands last, so it is what the listener remembers.
- Bold used for emphasis rather than for a term the reader will scan for. Bold is silent in audio, so any meaning it carries is lost in a third of the delivery surfaces. Port Madison uses none.

## Numbers to hit

Flesch reading ease 55-70; Port Madison's 70.2 is the model, 50 is a hard floor (89 of 95 entries currently fall below it). Average sentence 15-20 words, median at or below 17. Sentence-length standard deviation at or above 9 — this is the rhythm test and it is non-negotiable. At least 30 percent of sentences 12 words or shorter (exemplars: 34 and 32 percent; weak entries: zero). No sentence over 45 words, and no more than one sentence over 35 words per 1,000 words. Paragraphs 1-6 sentences, and never three consecutive paragraphs with the same sentence count. 4-6 sections, headings unique to this entry. Total body 650-900 words (exemplars: 666 and 868; weak entries: 1,049 and 1,150 — the extra length is all connective tissue). Abstract-noun density (-tion/-ment/-ance/-ity/-ness/-ship/-ism) at or below 4 per 100 words; Port Madison runs 0.9, the weak pair run 10.5 and 11.9. At least 15 numerals per 1,000 words, with at least one full Month-Day-Year date where the record supports it. Zero uses of 'while' as a conjunction. Zero stock evaluative adjectives from the banned list. Read-aloud check: no nested parentheses, no more than one comma-delimited aside per sentence, and every sentence speakable in a single breath.

## Rewrites in the voice

> **Before:** Perched on a bluff overlooking the scenic waters of Possession Sound, Mukilteo Lighthouse has served as a beloved beacon and community landmark for over a century.
>
> **After:** Mukilteo Lighthouse sits on a bluff above Possession Sound. It has been there more than a hundred years.
>
> 26 words to 18, one sentence to two, and the second runs 8 words for rhythm. Drops the participial opener, 'scenic', 'beloved', and 'has served as' — none of which is a fact anyone could check. Every noun that survives is in the original sentence.

> **Before:** The establishment of the Mukilteo-Clinton ferry route in the early 1900s represented a significant improvement in transportation reliability, allowing Whidbey Island residents to commute to mainland jobs, access medical services, and maintain family connections that had been difficult to sustain during the era of irregular boat service.
>
> **After:** The Mukilteo-Clinton ferry route opened in the early 1900s. Before it, Whidbey Island ran on irregular boats. After it, islanders could hold a job on the mainland, see a doctor, and keep up with family.
>
> 48 words in one breath becomes 35 across three sentences of 10, 8, and 17 words. The nominalized subject ('The establishment of') gets a verb, 'represented a significant improvement' is deleted as an unsourceable verdict, and the before/after structure carries the improvement without asserting it. No fact added.

> **Before:** Community volunteers contributed thousands of hours to restoration efforts while organizing fundraising activities that demonstrated extraordinary civic commitment to preserving local maritime heritage.
>
> **After:** Volunteers restored the building and raised the money to do it.
>
> 22 words to 11. Cuts the 'while' weld, the unverifiable count ('thousands of hours'), and the editorial verdict ('extraordinary civic commitment'). What remains is the only part that could be sourced. If the actual hour figure or fundraising total exists in a record, that number goes back in — a real number beats an intensity word every time.

> **Before:** In the heart of Washington's Yakima Valley, the Yakama Nation Museum stands as a powerful reflection of the enduring culture and history of the Confederated Tribes and Bands of the Yakama Nation.
>
> **After:** The Yakama Nation Museum sits in the Yakima Valley. It holds the history of the Confederated Tribes and Bands of the Yakama Nation.
>
> 31 words to 23, split 9 and 14. 'In the heart of' and 'stands as a powerful reflection of' are removed as booster tone — the accuracy standard bars romanticizing, and on this material particularly. Nothing is lost: the museum, the valley, the confederation, and the history are all still there, now stated plainly and proportionately.

> **Before:** The Yakama Nation Museum emerged from the cultural revitalization movements of the 1960s and 1970s, when many Native American communities began asserting greater control over how their histories and cultures were presented to both tribal members and the general public.
>
> **After:** The museum came out of the cultural revitalization years of the 1960s and 1970s. Native communities across the country were taking control of how their history got told, to their own members and to everyone else.
>
> 40 words to 36, split 15 and 21, and both are speakable in one breath. 'Emerged from' becomes 'came out of'; the long relative clause becomes its own sentence with a subject that acts. No claim is strengthened — 'many communities began asserting' becomes 'were taking', which is the same tense-neutral fact without the hedge-adverb clutter. If the sourcing supports naming the specific movement or year the museum opened, that number replaces the vague decade span.

## Accuracy — the part that is not stylistic

Every factual claim must trace to a graded source. **Wikipedia is never an authority**; it is a finding aid only.

| Tier | Examples |
|---|---|
| Primary / official | NPS, BLM, USFS, DOE, state park agencies, US Coast Guard, National Register nomination documents, Library of Congress, primary law, university-press primary editions |
| Peer-reviewed | Journals, university-press scholarship |
| Editorially reviewed secondary | HistoryLink.org, The Oregon Encyclopedia — citable, but **attribute in text** for anything contested |
| Tribal institutions | For their own history, cross-checked |
| Not acceptable | Wikipedia, Find a Grave, Historical Marker Database, enthusiast/tourism/booster sites, forums, AI-generated encyclopedias |

- Superlatives are **bounded or attributed**, never asserted flat.
- Anything unconfirmed is **marked unverified or dropped** — never repeated on secondary faith.
- Where the record contradicts itself, **say so in the text** and name both sources. That is content, not weakness.
- Neutrality: shared history, stated plainly and proportionately. No romanticising, no booster tone, no advocacy. Legends are labelled as legends. No manufactured balance for documented facts.
- Every entry ends with a `## Sources` block listing each source and its tier.

## Machine-checkable gate

These can all be enforced in CI before an entry merges:

- 650–900 words of body prose
- Flesch reading ease ≥ 50 (target 55–70)
- ≥ 30% of sentences at 12 words or fewer
- No sentence over 45 words
- Zero uses of "while" as a connective
- Zero banned adjectives; zero "stands as / serves as / represents / reflects / provides" as main verbs
- ≥ 15 numerals per 1,000 words, and at least one full Month-Day-Year date
- Section headings unique across the corpus
- No 12-word sequence shared with another entry
- A populated `## Sources` block and a populated `factcheck` front-matter record
